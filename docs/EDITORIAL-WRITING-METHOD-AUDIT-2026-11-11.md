## What the current method does well

1. Task-first framing: articles usually focus on one reader problem rather than a broad lifestyle topic.
2. Actionable sequence: strong examples use steps, quick plans, checklists and a sensible order.
3. Decision support: watt checks, humidity measurement, fan sizing, hardware-by-load selection and test interpretation are more useful than generic advice.
4. Limits and escalation: several guides explain what a tool cannot do and when to stop and call a professional.
5. Regional awareness: some technical guides distinguish US/UK/EU electrical or product differences.
6. Useful cross-linking: diagnosis and buying guides remain separate when intent differs.
7. Technical publication hygiene: the inventory reports all 30 indexable guides have canonical URLs, meta descriptions, Sources sections, dateModified, BlogPosting/Article schema and FAQPage schema.

## Weaknesses and improvement opportunities

1. Formula repetition: repeated sections such as "Quick win", "What not to buy yet", "Common mistakes", "Keep going" and long FAQs can make unrelated articles feel templated. Keep only sections that solve a real reader question.
2. Inconsistent direct answer: some articles start with a useful lead but no concise answer. Give the answer immediately when the query warrants it; do not add a redundant answer heading when the lead already does the job.
3. Sources too far from claims: critical numeric, safety, code, product-compatibility and diagnostic claims need nearby attribution. A Sources list alone is insufficient.
4. Uneven source quality: prioritize government agencies, standards bodies, extension services, manufacturer manuals and recognized technical research. Competitor articles reveal reader questions and SERP expectations, but should not be the authority for technical claims.
5. Image count is not value: several sample articles use six or more images, but count does not prove usefulness. Prefer one precise original diagram, annotated decision flow, measurement illustration or comparison table when it teaches something; remove decorative repetition.
6. Underused decision assets: symptom-to-cause-to-safe-check-to-result-to-next-step tables, calculators and printable checklists can create genuine utility.
7. Honest experience: never imply field testing, expert review or first-hand observation without evidence. Distinguish sourced facts from editorial synthesis.
8. Earned length: do not target 1,500, 2,000 or 3,000 words. Cover the task and its limits; remove repetition and generic filler.
9. FAQ value: FAQs should resolve remaining intent, not restate body paragraphs. Ensure schema matches visible content.
10. Internal links: add a small number at the point where the next decision arises, not a generic list of unrelated articles.

## Initial repository overlap decision

- where-to-put-water-leak-sensors is prevention, sensor placement, alerting and device limits; it does not currently provide a full diagnostic process for an existing suspected leak.
- reduce-bathroom-condensation already distinguishes condensation from leak clues. A new guide must link to it and avoid duplicating bathroom condensation treatment.
- how-to-fix-damp-crawl-space-humidity covers crawl-space water/moisture diagnosis and remediation, not whole-home plumbing diagnosis.
- bathroom-exhaust-fan-dripping-water is a specific bathroom-fan symptom guide.
- macerating-toilet-basement-bathroom is a buying/install decision, not a leak diagnosis guide.
- The repository contains no standalone article explicitly dedicated to whole-home hidden plumbing leak diagnosis.

## Upgraded writing method for future briefs

A. Start with the reader's decision, not a keyword outline. State the situation, required decision/action, successful outcome and scope boundary.

B. Build an evidence-backed decision path. For each step specify preparation, action, expected observation, what it means, what it does not prove, safe next step and stop/escalation condition.

C. Separate fact types. Distinguish sourced requirements, manufacturer instructions, local-code variations, estimates and editorial recommendations. Put sources near critical claims and include a compact source list.

D. Use differentiated assets. Add a diagram, table, calculator, checklist or worksheet only where it makes the job safer or easier. Prefer original explanatory diagrams over decorative AI imagery.

E. Design for scan and depth together. Use a direct answer when useful, meaningful question-led subheadings, concise steps, troubleshooting branches, limitations and a next action. Avoid forced template sections.

F. Use FAQs as a gap check. Derive them from unresolved intent and reader objections; remove duplication.

G. Run a competitor gap test. Ask what decision a reader can make more confidently with our page than with the top competitors. If the answer is not concrete, reframe or reject.

H. Run factual and safety review. Verify technical claims against primary documentation; check electrical, water, mold, structural and tool-related risks; make stop-work points unmistakable.

I. Simulate the reader. Can a homeowner act without guessing? Can they tell when a test is inconclusive? Can they identify the next safe action?

J. Review outcomes after publication. Use GSC queries, CTR, utility events where available and reader feedback. A single click is not proof of demand.
