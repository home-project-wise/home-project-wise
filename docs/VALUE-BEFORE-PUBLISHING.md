# Value Before Publishing — Mandatory Operating Law

**Version:** 1.0  
**Scope:** All websites and projects, every page, tool, article, feature, and search-engine-optimization (SEO) change.  
**Status:** Mandatory.  
**Core rule:** We do not publish merely because a page is technically ready. We publish when it provides real visitor value, has passed appropriate quality checks, and there is a reasonable, evidence-based case that it deserves to appear in search results.

## Article 1 — Primary objective

Our objective is to build useful websites that can attract visitors from search engines consistently and develop over time toward sustainable revenue.

Every task must serve one or more of these goals:

- Solve a real visitor problem.
- Answer a question people are trying to answer.
- Save visitors time, money, or effort.
- Help visitors make a better decision.
- Provide a practical tool, trustworthy information, or a better experience than available alternatives.
- Help the site reach the right audience and turn visits into useful engagement, then revenue when appropriate for the project.

A large page count, a high line count, or successful indexing alone is not evidence of project success.

## Article 2 — Identify real demand before development

Before creating a page, tool, or feature, identify:

1. Who is the target visitor?
2. What problem or question do they want to solve?
3. Why might they search for this topic?
4. What available evidence indicates genuine interest?
5. What format is best suited to solving the problem?

Use available evidence such as search results, search suggestions, competitor pages, Google Search Console, Bing Webmaster Tools, recurring questions, and specialist sources.

If sufficient data is unavailable, record the idea as a hypothesis to test. Never invent search volume, demand figures, or traffic forecasts.

**Rule:** Start with the visitor's need, not with a desire to increase page count.

## Article 3 — Study competitors before building the solution

Before developing an important page or tool, review direct competitors and alternatives the audience may use.

Depending on the project, record:

- Actual URLs of the pages reviewed.
- The date of review.
- What each alternative does well.
- Verifiable weaknesses or gaps.
- What visitors need but are not adequately getting.
- The realistic opportunity our site could address.
- The meaningful differences our solution will offer.

Do not copy a competitor's design, content, or structure simply because it ranks well. Learn from its strengths and look for an opportunity to deliver greater value.

Never claim to have reviewed a specific number of competitors unless those reviews were actually completed.

## Article 4 — Define added value before writing code

Before implementation, the team must answer clearly:

**Why would a visitor choose our page instead of the pages already available?**

Identify a tangible benefit, such as:

- A more accurate or clearer calculation.
- Easier, actionable steps.
- A comparison that supports a decision.
- More current information or better-supported claims.
- Practical examples that show how to apply the advice.
- A faster, easier experience on a phone.
- Handling an important case that alternatives overlook.
- Bringing essential information and steps together without overwhelming the visitor.

Every page does not need to be revolutionary or better than every competitor in every respect. It must, however, have a real and convincing reason to exist.

If that benefit cannot be identified, reconsider the idea before development.

## Article 5 — Choose the format that solves the problem

Do not force every topic into one format. Choose what best serves the visitor, for example:

- An interactive calculator or tool.
- A practical step-by-step guide.
- A comparison of options.
- A checklist.
- Examples and use cases.
- A cost table or estimates.
- A question-and-answer page.
- A combination of a tool and explanatory content.

If a tool solves the problem better than an article, build the tool. If a practical guide is more appropriate, write a useful guide.

Do not create articles or tools merely to target keywords without delivering genuine value.

## Article 6 — Content quality and accuracy

Every fact, recommendation, formula, and financial estimate must be as accurate as the available evidence permits.

The team must:

- Use reliable sources appropriate to the topic.
- Prefer official or primary sources when relevant.
- Explain assumptions and calculation methods.
- State limitations and uncertainty when they affect interpretation.
- Check the freshness of information that changes over time.
- Correct inaccurate information when discovered.
- Never fabricate numbers, prices, statistics, citations, or test results.
- Never claim personal experience or testing that did not occur.
- Never promise guaranteed results or earnings.

When evidence is insufficient, disclose that limitation instead of presenting a guess as fact.

## Article 7 — Prevent thin and duplicate content

Do not publish pages whose primary purpose is to increase the number of indexed pages without adding distinct value.

Prohibited patterns include:

- Superficially rewriting competitors' content.
- Producing near-identical pages by changing only city names or keywords without a real difference in usefulness.
- Writing generic filler text.
- Repeating the same information across multiple pages without a clear need.
- Keyword stuffing.
- Creating pages that fail to satisfy their intended search intent.
- Producing large volumes of AI-generated content without review or added value.

AI may be used for research, analysis, writing, and programming. The final result must still be useful, accurate, and sufficiently original to serve visitors.

If a page is weak, improve it, consolidate it with a more suitable page, or abandon it rather than publishing it just to complete a plan.

## Article 8 — Tool quality and user experience

For calculators, tools, and interactive interfaces, visual polish alone is not sufficient.

Verify, as applicable:

- Calculation and output correctness.
- Input validity and boundaries.
- Handling of empty, zero, and illogical values.
- Clear error messages and instructions for recovery.
- Explanations of important results and assumptions.
- Usability on phones and small screens.
- Accessibility of essential controls.
- Clarity of labels, headings, and results.
- Performance and absence of errors that break functionality.
- Appropriate handling of edge cases.
- Expected behavior of core features.

Never report a test as passed if it was not run. Never hide a known problem merely because the page looks correct.

## Article 9 — SEO is part of quality, not a substitute for it

After confirming the page's value, review its discoverability and indexing readiness. Where relevant, check:

- A clear title that reflects the page topic.
- A suitable description of its content.
- Well-organized headings.
- Useful internal links between related pages.
- Correct canonical URLs.
- Search-engine accessibility.
- A valid sitemap.
- Correct structured data when appropriate.
- Relevant images and useful alternative text.
- Broken links and unnecessary redirects.
- Suitable mobile performance and user experience.
- Duplicate-page and technical issues that prevent discovery or indexing.

Do not use structured data, keywords, or internal links deceptively or artificially.

**Technical indexing readiness is a supporting condition; it does not guarantee rankings, traffic, or revenue.**

## Article 10 — Internal links and site structure

The site's structure must help visitors and search engines understand topics and find important pages.

Therefore:

- Link related pages when the links genuinely help.
- Use clear anchor text that describes the destination.
- Ensure important pages are not isolated from the rest of the site.
- Organize tools and guides around audience needs.
- Do not add links merely to increase their count.
- Check that links work and lead to appropriate destinations.

The purpose of links is to improve navigation, understanding, and access to value—not to manipulate rankings.

## Article 11 — Mandatory pre-publication quality review

Every new page, tool, or feature must pass a review gate before publication.

### Required checklist

- [ ] The target audience and problem are identified.
- [ ] There is reasonable evidence of audience interest, or the idea is explicitly recorded as a hypothesis.
- [ ] Relevant alternatives have been reviewed without overstating the scope of the research.
- [ ] The page's added value is defined.
- [ ] The page is not filler, weak duplication, or a superficial rewrite.
- [ ] Important facts, formulas, examples, and sources have been checked.
- [ ] Assumptions and limitations that affect interpretation are explained.
- [ ] Relevant functionality and edge cases have been tested.
- [ ] Mobile experience, accessibility, and performance have been reviewed as appropriate.
- [ ] Relevant SEO, indexing, and link elements have been reviewed.
- [ ] Tests actually performed and items still untested are documented.
- [ ] A suitable post-publication measure has been defined.

Do not waive a material problem with accuracy, usefulness, or functional safety just to meet a deadline or increase publication volume.

## Article 12 — Required contents of every pull request

Every pull request that changes visitor-facing content or functionality, or affects SEO, must explain:

1. **Need:** What problem are we solving?
2. **Evidence:** What supports the need, or why is the hypothesis reasonable?
3. **Value:** What new benefit will the visitor receive?
4. **Differentiation:** How does the solution differ from relevant alternatives?
5. **Verification:** What tests and checks were actually run?
6. **Limitations:** What remains untested or uncertain?
7. **Follow-up:** How will we later determine whether the change was useful?

Pure documentation changes may state that application tests were not required or were not run, and explain why.

Passing CI is not proof of market demand; evidence of demand is not proof that implementation is bug-free.

## Article 13 — Post-publication monitoring

After publication, review actual results when sufficient data is available.

Depending on the site, useful measures may include:

- Search-result impressions.
- Clicks from search engines.
- Click-through rate relative to impressions.
- Queries that bring visitors.
- Pages attracting relevant visits.
- Tool use and successful task completion.
- Errors or difficulties visitors encounter.
- Conversions or revenue when suitable measurement is available.

Interpret data cautiously. A rise or fall may have multiple causes; do not assert a cause without appropriate evidence.

If a page receives substantial impressions but few clicks, review its title, description, and search intent. If it attracts visits without useful engagement, review whether the content meets visitor expectations and whether the experience works well. If it does not appear in search, investigate indexing, demand, competition, and quality before deciding what to do.

Do not treat a fixed waiting period as a guarantee of success. Review according to the amount of data available and the nature of the topic.

## Article 14 — Evidence-led continuous improvement

Work does not end at publication.

Use actual results to decide what to:

- Improve.
- Expand.
- Update.
- Redesign.
- Link to other pages.
- Consolidate with other content.
- Stop developing when its value is no longer clear.

Prioritize work that combines a real need, demonstrable value, and a credible opportunity to grow rather than distributing effort equally across all pages.

Do not produce dozens of similar pages in response to weak results before diagnosing the problem.

## Article 15 — Responsibility and autonomy

Everyone working on a project—including ChatGPT, Claude, people, and assisting tools—must follow this law.

Each contributor must:

- Read the law before relevant work begins.
- Apply it in research, planning, development, and review.
- Document evidence, decisions, and tests.
- Record what is done, what is not done, and what the next person needs.
- Never claim completion or verification that did not happen.
- Respect repository permissions and established working rules.
- Avoid dangerous or irreversible changes without the required authorization.

Routine, low-risk decisions may be made independently within available permissions. Decisions requiring the owner's permission or materially changing project direction must be escalated.

## Article 16 — Final decision rule

Before publishing any work, we must answer clearly:

1. What real need does it serve?
2. What tangible value does it provide to the visitor?
3. Why is it worth choosing over available alternatives?

If these questions cannot be answered with reasonable evidence, do not publish the work in its current form. Research further, improve the idea, defer it, or abandon it.

Never promise the owner or visitor guaranteed traffic or revenue. Improve the likelihood of success, measure outcomes, and learn from real evidence.

## Final commitment

**Value before publishing. Evidence before claims. Quality before scale. Measurement before declaring success.**

Every page must deserve the visitor's time. Every development task must serve a clear purpose. Every decision must improve the chance of building a useful, growable site that can generate revenue without confusing activity with results.
